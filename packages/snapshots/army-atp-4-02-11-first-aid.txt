## HUMAN OR ANIMAL BITES
16-5. First aid for human or animal bites aims to prevent infection and provide initial care for the wound. To
perform first aid for a human or animal bite:
• Remove yourself from further harm. If dealing with an animal bite, ensure that you are no longer at
risk of being bitten again.
• Control bleeding. If the bite wound is bleeding, apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or sterile
gauze to stop or minimize the bleeding. Elevating the wound, if possible, can also help reduce
bleeding.
• Clean the wound. Rinse the bite wound thoroughly with clean water or saline solution to flush out
any dirt, bacteria, or saliva that may have entered the wound. Gently remove any debris from the
wound using mild soap and water.
• Apply an antiseptic. After cleaning, apply an antiseptic solution or ointment to the wound to help
reduce the risk of infection. Follow the instructions on the product label and ensure it is suitable for
use on open wounds.
• Cover the wound. Use a sterile dressing or clean bandage to cover the wound. This will protect it
from further contamination and promote healing. Change dressing regularly to maintain cleanliness.
• Seek medical attention. It is important to seek medical evaluation for all animal bites, especially
those from unfamiliar or wild animals, deep wounds, or those that become red, swollen, or
increasingly painful. A health care professional can assess the severity of the bite, prescribe
antibiotics if necessary, and administer tetanus prophylaxis if required.

## FIRST AID FOR A SNAKEBITE
16-40. In the event of a snakebite, prompt first aid is crucial to minimizing the potential effects of the venom.
To perform first aid for a snakebite:
• Stay calm and call for medical assistance immediately. Provide medical personnel with information
about the snake, if possible, but do not attempt to capture or kill the snake as it may increase the risk
of further bites.
• Keep the bitten Service member as still and calm as possible. Restrict movement and avoid
unnecessary exertion, as it can accelerate the spread of venom in the bloodstream.
• Remove any constricting items, such as jewelry or tight clothing, from the affected area, as swelling
may occur.
• Position the affected limb slightly below the level of the heart, if possible. This can help slow the
spread of venom.
• Cleanse the wound gently with mild soap and water to reduce the risk of infection. Do not apply ice
or use a TQ, as they are not effective and can cause additional harm.
• Apply a sterile bandage or dress over the bite, ensuring it is snug but not too tight. This can help
immobilize the area and reduce the spread of venom.
• Encourage the person to remain calm and still, while waiting for medical help to arrive. Monitor
their vital signs, such as breathing and heart rate, and be prepared to administer CPR if necessary.

## TICKS
16-78. A tick is a small arachnid parasite that feeds on the blood of animals, including humans. Ticks are
found in various environments, such as forests, grasslands, and shrubby areas. They latch onto their hosts
and can transmit diseases through their bites.
16-79. When a tick bites, it burrows its mouth parts into the skin to feed on blood. If you discover a tick
attached to your skin, it's important to remove it promptly and take the following first aid steps:
• Prepare for tick removal: Use fine-tipped tweezers to grasp the tick as close to the skin's surface as
possible. Avoid squeezing or crushing the tick's body.
• Remove the tick: Gently and steadily pull the tick upward with even pressure. Do not twist or jerk
it. The goal is to remove the entire tick, including its mouthparts. Dispose of the tick by placing it
in a sealed bag or container or by submerging it in alcohol.
• Clean the bite area: Thoroughly clean the bite area with soap and water or an antiseptic solution to
reduce the risk of infection.

## HEAT INJURIES
17-5. Heat injury prevention is of utmost importance in the military, where Service members face intense
physical challenges and operate in diverse climatic conditions. When the body gets overheated, it can lead to
serious health risks and impair a Service member’s ability to perform their duties effectively. Heat-related
injuries, such as heat exhaustion and heat stroke, can be prevented through various measures, including
wearing appropriate clothing, taking regular rest breaks, acclimatization and fluid replacement. By
prioritizing heat injury prevention, Leaders ensure the well-being and operational readiness of their
personnel, allowing them to fulfill their responsibilities and accomplish their missions with strength and
resilience.
17-6. Exertional heat illness refers to a spectrum of heat-related conditions that occur when the body's core
temperature rises excessively during physical activity or exercise. It is primarily caused by prolonged exertion
in hot and humid environments, leading to an imbalance between heat production and heat dissipation.
Exertional heat illness encompasses various conditions ranging from mild heat exhaustion to severe heat
stroke, which is a medical emergency. Symptoms of heat injuries may include:
• Fatigue.
• Dizziness.
• Headache.
• Nausea.
• Excessive sweating.
• Rapid heartbeat.
• Confusion.
• Loss of consciousness in severe cases.
17-7. Prompt recognition and treatment of exertional heat illness are essential to prevent complications and
potentially life-threatening consequences. Effective prevention strategies, such as acclimatization,
appropriate clothing, fluid replacement, and regular rest breaks, are crucial in minimizing the risk of
exertional heat illness. For more information on heat illness refer to TB MED 507 and TC 4-02.3.
17-8. While there is a range of adverse effects that can result from the body overheating, the two major kinds
of heat illnesses that are referred to as heat casualties are heat exhaustion (can be mild or more severe) or
heat stroke. Heat stroke is the most severe form of heat illness and possibly fatal.
Note. For more information on heat illness prevention, refer to the Defense Centers for Public
Health – Aberdeen website.

## HEAT EXHAUSTION
17-9. Heat exhaustion is a heat-related condition that occurs when the body becomes overheated due to
prolonged exposure to high temperatures and inadequate fluid replacement. It is considered a milder form of
heat illness compared to heat stroke. Signs and symptoms of heat exhaustion may include:
• Heavy sweating.

• Weaknesses and fatigue.
• Dizziness or lightheadedness.
• Headache.
• Nausea or vomiting.
• Pale, cool, or clammy skin.
• Rapid and shallow breathing.
• Elevated heart rate.
• Muscle cramps.
• Feeling faint or fainting.
17-10. If a Service member is suspected to have heat exhaustion, immediate first aid treatment should be
provided. The first aid steps to treat heat exhaustion are:
• Move the Service member to a cooler environment: Get them out of the heat and into a shaded or
air-conditioned area.
• Encourage rest and rehydration: Have the Service member lie down and elevate their legs slightly.
Provide them with cool water and or an electrolyte-containing sports drink to sip on. Avoid alcohol
or caffeine.
• Cool the Service member's body: Loosen or remove any tight or unnecessary clothing. Apply a cool,
wet cloth or towels to their skin, especially on the neck, face, and armpits. Use a fan or create air
movement to aid in evaporation.
• Monitor their condition: Stay with the Service member and observe their symptoms. If they do not
improve within 30 minutes or their condition worsens, seek medical assistance immediately.
17-11. Heat exhaustion can progress to heat stroke, a life-threatening condition. If the person's symptoms
worsen, they become disoriented, confused, or lose consciousness, immediately call for medical assistance.
17-12. Prevention is key in avoiding heat exhaustion. Practice proper fluid replacement, especially when
engaging in physical activity or spending time in hot environments. Wear lightweight and loose-fitting
clothing, use sunscreen, and take regular breaks in cool areas to avoid prolonged exposure to heat.
17-13. Heat exhaustion is often preceded by heat cramps, muscle cramps of the arms, legs, or abdomen. Heat
cramps and heat exhaustion often act as indicators. These conditions need to be identified and treated before
they get to an extreme case of heat stroke. Catch these conditions early, as the casualty needs rest, water,
shade, evaluation, and possible medical care.

## HEAT STROKE
17-14. Heat stroke is a severe and potentially life-threatening illness characterized by a rapid increase in body
temperature. It occurs when the body's cooling mechanisms fail to regulate internal heat, often because of
prolonged exposure to high temperatures or physical exertion in hot environments. Heat stroke is a medical
emergency that requires immediate intervention. The signs and symptoms of heat stroke may include:
• Altered mental state or confusion.
• Headache, dizziness, or fainting.
• Nausea or vomiting.
• Difficulty breathing.
• Hot, dry skin (lack of sweating).
• High body temperature (typically above 104°Fahrenheit or 40°Celsius).
• Rapid and strong heartbeat.
• Seizures.
17-15. If someone is suspected of having heat stroke, it is crucial to seek emergency medical help
immediately. While waiting for medical professionals to arrive, follow these first aid steps:
• Move the person to a cooler location: Get them out of the heat and into a shaded or air-conditioned
area as quickly as possible.
• Call for emergency assistance: Dial 911 or the appropriate local emergency number to request
immediate medical help to include MEDEVAC.

• Monitor their vital signs: Keep a close eye on the person's breathing, pulse, and level of

## HYPONATREMIA (WATER INTOXICATION)
17-18. Hyponatremia (water intoxication) is a condition characterized by abnormally low levels of sodium
in the blood. Sodium plays a crucial role in maintaining the body's fluid balance and proper nerve and muscle
function. When sodium levels become significantly diluted, usually due to excessive water intake or
inadequate sodium intake, hyponatremia can occur. The signs and symptoms of hyponatremia can vary
depending on the severity of the condition. Common manifestations include:
• Nausea and vomiting.
• Headache.
• Confusion or altered mental state.
• Fatigue and weakness.
• Seizures.
• Muscle cramps or spasms.
• Loss of appetite.
• Swelling in the hands, feet, or legs.
• Low blood pressure.
• Coma (in severe cases).
17-19. If someone is suspected to have hyponatremia, it is important to seek medical attention. In the
meantime, you can provide the following first aid measures:
• Assess the person's condition: Check for any signs of severe symptoms such as seizures, loss of
consciousness, or difficulty breathing. If any of these are present, call emergency services
immediately.
• Move the person to a cool, shaded area: Ensure they are away from direct sunlight or heat sources.
• Encourage fluid intake: If the person is conscious and able to swallow, give them small sips of a
beverage containing electrolytes, such as a sports drink. This can help restore sodium levels.
• Do not give more water. Providing additional water will make the casualty more hyponatremic.
• If awake, allow the Service member to consume salty foods or snacks.
• Monitor the person's vital signs: Keep an eye on their breathing, heart rate, and level of
consciousness. If their condition worsens, be prepared to administer CPR if necessary.
17-20. Hyponatremia can be mistaken for heat stroke, and the treatment is very different. Hyponatremia is a
medical emergency that requires professional diagnosis and treatment. It is important to address the
underlying cause and restore sodium levels under the guidance of a health care professional.
Note. This condition most often occurs during initial entry training; however, it may occur anytime
when overhydration is encountered.

## HIGH-ALTITUDE PULMONARY EDEMA
17-52. High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema is a serious condition that can occur at higher elevations, typically
above 2,500 meters (8,200 feet). It involves the accumulation of fluid fluids in the lungs and can be life-
threatening. Some general first aid measures for Service members experiencing HAPE in mountainous terrain
include:
• Immediate Descent. The most critical first aid measure for HAPE is an immediate descent to lower
altitudes. Every effort should be made to get the affected individual to a lower elevation as quickly
as possible, as this is the most effective way to alleviate symptoms.
• Rest and Oxygen. Ensure the person rests and provide supplemental oxygen if available. Oxygen
therapy can help improve oxygen levels in the blood and alleviate breathing difficulties.
• Keep Warm. Maintain the person's warmth by using blankets and insulating layers. Cold
temperatures can exacerbate symptoms, so preventing hypothermia is important.
• Stay Hydrated. Encourage the individual to drink fluids to stay hydrated. Dehydration can worsen
the condition, so maintaining adequate fluid intake is essential.
• Monitor Breathing. Keep a close watch on the person's breathing. If there are signs of severe
respiratory distress, such as blue lips or fingertips, it may indicate a critical situation, and
professional medical help should be sought immediately.
• Administer Medications (if available). In some cases, medications such as nifedipine or sildenafil
may be used to help manage HAPE symptoms. The medical professional should determine the
availability and use of these medications.
• Evacuation. Seek evacuation to the MTF at a lower altitude as soon as possible. Evacuation may
involve contacting medical personnel and requesting evacuation to a lower elevation where more
definitive medical care can be provided.
17-53. HAPE is a medical emergency, and prompt action is crucial. While these first aid measures can
provide initial assistance, professional medical help should be sought without delay. If you suspect someone
has HAPE, it's essential to prioritize their safety and act swiftly to get them to a lower altitude and seek
immediate medical attention.

on the brain.

Oxygen therapy can help improve oxygen levels in the blood and alleviate neurological symptoms.
